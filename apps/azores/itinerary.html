One small island,
nine days.

São Miguel is the largest of nine islands sitting alone in the middle of the Atlantic, about a third of the way from Portugal to North America. It is green in a way that is difficult to photograph, built out of volcanoes, and small enough that you can cross it in an afternoon. Three places to sleep, a car, and a mostly empty calendar.

Nights 1–4 Ponta Delgada Nights 5–6 Furnas Nights 7–8 North coast Team: Alex & Jackie

The one rule

The weather on São Miguel changes fast and it changes locally. It can be sunny where we are standing and cloudy at a crater lake twenty-five minutes away. So rather than lock the days down in advance, we look at the forecast the night before and decide in the morning. Only a handful of things have a fixed time on them. The rest of the trip moves around the good weather — if a day looks grey, we swap it, and nothing is lost.

Where we're staying

Nights 1–4 · Aug 24–28

Vila Galé Collection S. Miguel

Campo de São Francisco, Ponta Delgada

A nineteenth-century building on the main square in the old capital, across from a sixteenth-century sea fort. A convent, then the city hospital for a hundred years, before it became a hotel — they kept the original plasterwork and tile floors. Courtyard pool, indoor pool, spa with sauna and steam room. Junior Suite with a balcony, breakfast included.

+351 296 240 430 · call
Nights 5–6 · Aug 28–30

Terra Nostra Garden Hotel

Rua Padre José Jacinto Botelho 5, Furnas

Furnas is a village in a valley where the ground steams. Terra Nostra sits next to a botanical garden built in the 1770s, in the middle of which is a large thermal pool the colour of strong tea, heated by the volcano and full of iron. Guests can use the garden and pool twenty-four hours a day — including early and late, when the day visitors have gone.

+351 296 549 090 · call

On the cliffs facing the open Atlantic, with a sea-view room, a spa, a pool and a restaurant on site. Twenty minutes from the airport, so the last morning is easy. Deliberately the quiet end of the trip — by then we will have been moving for a week.

The Newark connection on the way out is quick, so anything we would badly miss should ride in a carry-on. The Azores are seven hours ahead of California, and we lose the night on the plane. A small automatic SUV is waiting at the airport when we land, ours for the whole week. There is no ride-share on the island and buses only really serve the capital, so the car is how the trip works.

The roads are narrow and often have stone walls right at the edge, so it is slow going and that is fine. Cows genuinely do stand in the road, especially around the crater lakes and in the east. Fog can come down fast on the high roads.

Eating

Try at least once

  • Cozido das Furnas — the stew cooked in volcanic ground. We have this on Sunday.
  • Lapas grelhadas — limpets grilled in garlic butter, served in the shell with a lemon. Order them whenever they appear.
  • Bolo lêvedo — a sweet muffin cooked on a griddle, from Furnas. Best warm from a village bakery.
  • Queijadas da Vila — small cheese tarts from Vila Franca do Campo.
  • Azorean pineapple — grown in greenhouses over two years, and unlike any pineapple you have had.

Worth planning around

  • Restaurante da Associação Agrícola, Ponta Delgada — run by the island's farming association, widely held to be the best beef in the Azores. Needs a reservation a few days out.
  • Bar Caloura — on the rocks above the water east of the city. Pick your fish from the day's catch. No reservations, so go early.
  • Alcides, Ponta Delgada — the local institution for steak.
  • A Tasca, Ponta Delgada — small plates, busy and local, good for a first-day lunch.
  • Ponta do Garajau, Ribeira Quente — rustic, half-outdoor, known island-wide for prawns and limpets.

What to pack

Specific to here

  • A dark swimsuit and a dark towel. The thermal water is full of iron and permanently stains anything pale. We will be in it four or five times.
  • A bag for wet swimwear — we will be in and out of pools all week.
  • Shoes with real grip. The trails are muddy even in August.
  • A light rain shell. Rain comes in short bursts and the wind ruins umbrellas.
  • A light layer for evenings and anywhere high up.
  • Sunscreen — the sun is stronger than the temperature makes it feel.
  • A mask and snorkel, for the islet.

The ordinary things

  • Late August is low seventies in the day, high sixties at night, and the ocean is warm enough to swim without thinking about it.
  • Nowhere we are going has a dress code. One nicer outfit each is plenty.
  • A European plug adapter.
  • Passports, valid at least three months past our return.

Good to know

Practical

  • Portugal uses the euro. Cards work almost everywhere, but carry some cash for village cafés and parking.
  • Tipping is light — rounding up, or five to ten percent for a good dinner.
  • Portuguese is the language, but English is widely spoken. There are long-standing family ties between the Azores and California.
  • An eSIM for the week costs very little. Coverage is good along the coast and patchy inland, so download offline maps before we go.
  • The Azores are among the safest places in Europe.
